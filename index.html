Project 01 · Finished

How the brain responds to sounds and pictures

A full EEG analysis in Python with MNE: load a real recording, clean it, cut it into short windows around each sound and picture, average the windows, and test whether the two responses differ.

The question

When a person hears a sound or sees a picture, does the scalp signal look different, and how soon after the event does the difference show?

What I did

  • Filtered the recording to remove slow drifts and fast noise, and repaired the bad sensors.
  • Re-referenced to the average of all sensors, so no single sensor is the zero point.
  • Cut the signal into short windows (epochs) around each sound and each picture, then averaged them. Averaging keeps what repeats every time and cancels random noise. The result is the evoked response.
  • Measured how strong the response is across the whole scalp over time (global field power) and drew scalp maps at the peaks.
  • Ran a permutation test: shuffle the sound and picture labels 5000 times and see how often chance alone gives a difference this big.

What I found

  • The sound response peaks near 97 ms. The picture response peaks later, near 187 ms.
  • The scalp pattern flips between the two: sounds are negative over the upper-central scalp, pictures are positive toward the front.
  • The difference in the 150 to 220 ms window is unlikely to be chance (p < 0.001).

Limits

One person, 20 seconds, 11 windows per condition. The analysis window was chosen after looking at the data, so the p-value is optimistic. Later projects fix their choices before looking.

Project 02 · Finished

Which ear heard the sound?

Decoding the side of a sound from brain signals, and finding out how soon after the sound that information shows up.

The question

Given only the brain signal, can a computer tell whether a sound went to the left or the right ear? And at what moment after the sound is that possible?

What I did

  • Used the full sample recording that ships with MNE.
  • Removed eye blinks with ICA, a method that splits the signal into independent sources so the blink source can be dropped.
  • Compared left and right responses with a cluster permutation test, which checks groups of neighbouring sensors and time points together instead of one at a time.
  • Trained a sliding decoder: a classifier trained again at every time point, so you see when the information appears.
  • Shuffled the left and right labels and repeated the decoding, to see what chance looks like.

What I found

  • Left vs right differ in a cluster of sensors and times (p = 0.004).
  • Decoding score (ROC AUC, where 0.5 is guessing and 1.0 is perfect): average 0.576, best 0.786 at 75 ms.
  • Against shuffled labels, the decoding is better than chance (p ≤ 0.01).

Limits

The average score is only a little above guessing. The strong part is a short window early after the sound. The best moment is the best of many time points, so on its own it overstates. The sample recording comes from one person, so this says nothing yet about people in general.

Project 03 · Finished

Which single neurons prefer a direction?

Single-neuron analysis on public mouse recordings: score every neuron, test each one against chance, and correct for running hundreds of tests.

The question

Some neurons fire more for one direction of motion than for the others. In one recording session, which neurons do that, and in which brain areas?

What I did

  • Loaded one session from the Allen Brain Observatory Neuropixels data (DANDI dandiset 000021) with pynwb.
  • Started with 1603 recorded units and kept the 452 that pass quality filters.
  • Gave each unit a direction selectivity index: how much more it fires for its favourite direction than for the others.
  • Tested each unit with a label shuffle: scramble which trials had which direction and see how high the index gets by luck.
  • Applied a Benjamini-Hochberg correction. When you test 452 neurons, some pass by luck, and this keeps the false discovery rate under control.

What I found

  • 29 of 452 units are direction-tuned (q < 0.05).
  • Visual cortex: 20 of 218 units. Hippocampus: 0 of 125.

Limits

One session from one animal. Finding no tuned units in hippocampus means none were detected here, not that none exist. The quality filters change the denominator, so the counts depend on them.
Project 04 · Finished

Can a quantum state survive in a warm brain?

Some researchers suggest that quantum effects inside neurons help produce consciousness. Others say the brain is too warm and wet for a quantum state to last long enough to matter. I tested that question with a simulation on an ordinary computer.

The question

How fast does a quantum state lose its quantum behaviour (decoherence) at body temperature, compared with how fast neurons work?

What I did

  • Built one quantum bit (qubit) from scratch in NumPy.
  • Added a warm environment around it with a Lindblad master equation, the standard way to describe a quantum system that leaks into its surroundings.
  • Measured how fast the quantum behaviour fades at body temperature.
  • Compared the result with the published argument by Tegmark and with the replies of his critics.

What I found

The measured fading rate, the comparison, and every assumption are in the README.

Limits

A single-qubit toy model is not a brain. Quantum consciousness is not established science, and this project does not settle it. It tests one argument against one model.

Project 05 · Finished

Alpha waves, eyes open and closed

A group-level test of one of the oldest findings in EEG, with every analysis choice written down before any result was looked at.

The question

Alpha waves are brain rhythms that swing 8 to 12 times per second, strongest at the back of the head. Is alpha power higher with the eyes closed than with the eyes open?

What I did

  • Used the PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/Imagery dataset: 64 sensors at 160 Hz, with a one-minute eyes-open baseline and a one-minute eyes-closed baseline per person.
  • Wrote a plan first and committed it: the band (8 to 12 Hz), the sensors (O1, Oz, O2 at the back of the head), one alpha number per person per condition, and a paired test.
  • Computed a power spectrum with Welch's method (many short pieces, averaged) and took the mean power in the band.
  • Compared the two conditions within each person, then reported the effect size, not only the p-value.

What I found

The result, the effect size and the number of people used are in the write-up.

Limits

The eyes-open recording always came first and the eyes-closed one second, so condition and order cannot be separated. Alpha at rest also differs a lot between people, which is why the comparison is paired.
Project 06 · In progress

Decoding a brain you have not seen

A benchmark of how brain-signal decoders hold up when they are tested on a different session, or a different person, from the one they trained on.

The question

A decoder that works well for the person it learned from often does worse on someone new. How much worse, and does a small deep model lose less than a classical one?

The design, locked before running

  • Data: the BNCI2014_001 motor imagery dataset through MOABB, 9 people with 2 sessions each. Task: imagining left hand vs right hand.
  • Classical pipeline: CSP with LDA, plus a covariance-based baseline.
  • Deep model: EEGNet, a small neural network built for EEG.
  • Three tests: within one session, across sessions of the same person, and across people.
  • Main score: ROC AUC. Comparison: paired per-person statistics and an effect size.
  • No changes to the design after the first run.

What I found

Nothing yet. The design is fixed and the analysis has not been run.

Limits

Nine people is a small sample, so results will carry wide uncertainty.
Project 07 · Finished

A simulated fly brain, from its wiring diagram

A connectome is a map of which neuron connects to which. I turned the public fruit fly map into a simulation and asked whether the wiring alone is enough to make a motor neuron respond to sugar.

The question

When the 32 sugar-sensing neurons are switched on, do the two motor neurons that extend the fly's tongue (MN9) fire, and is that because of the real wiring? There is no direct connection from the sugar neurons to MN9 in the data, so the signal has to pass through other neurons first.

What I did

  • Built a table of 14.3 million signed connections between 139,255 neurons from the FlyWire connectome. The sign (go or stop) comes from each neuron's predicted transmitter, and the strength from the number of synapses.
  • Simulated every neuron as a leaky integrate-and-fire cell in Brian2: signals fill it up, it slowly leaks, and it fires when it passes a line. Parameters follow papers that describe the Shiu et al. 2024 model.
  • Wrote the design, the controls and the rule for calling a result before running the full experiment.
  • Ran 83 simulations of one second each: the real sugar stimulus, 32 random sensory neurons, shuffled wiring (same connection strengths, targets scrambled), only the 24 acetylcholine sugar neurons, and a no-stimulus check.

What I found

  • With the sugar neurons on, MN9 fired in all 20 runs. With no stimulus it never fired.
  • With 32 random sensory neurons on, MN9 was almost silent (about 3 Hz on average, at most 69 Hz). With shuffled wiring it never fired, in all 20 runs. Every real run was above every control run.
  • So in this model the specific wiring carries the effect, not just the connection strengths.

Limits

The firing rates are not realistic (about 287 Hz in the model), so only the comparisons mean something. It is one fly brain, the connection strengths are guessed from synapse counts, MN9 is identified by a name match, and the random control is weak because many random neurons were visual. This is not a reproduction of the published paper.

Project 08 · Finished

When does each frequency appear?

A tool that shows how the frequency content of a signal changes over time, built two ways and checked against a signal with a known answer before it touched real data.

The question

Does my tool show the right frequency at the right time, and how does the choice of window change what it can see? The project started from claims that microtubules vibrate from kilohertz up to terahertz. Those claims are not established, and I found no public raw recording of them. So this tests the tool, not the claims.

What I did

  • Built a fake signal: 50 Hz for two seconds, then 200 Hz for two seconds. I wrote the pass rule first: peak frequencies within 5%, switch time within one window length.
  • Computed a spectrogram with the short-time Fourier transform (STFT), which slides one window along the signal, and with a wavelet transform, which uses longer windows for slow waves and shorter ones for fast waves.
  • Measured the blur of each method in time and in frequency, not just by eye.
  • Repeated the analysis on a real terahertz time-domain waveform (the dry-air baseline run) with a new rule written before looking: the strongest moment must fall within one window length of the largest sample.

What I found

  • Both methods found the right frequencies on the fake signal. Errors: 1.6% and 0.4% for the STFT, 2.8% and 2.0% for the wavelet.
  • The STFT blur is the same everywhere: 0.178 s and 5.1 Hz. The wavelet blur changes with frequency: 0.044 s and 21.6 Hz at 50 Hz, 0.011 s and 86.2 Hz at 200 Hz.
  • Time blur times frequency blur stays near 0.9 to 0.95 for both. You can move the blur between time and frequency, not remove it. Neither method is better.
  • On the real recording the pulse peaks at about 0.6 to 1 THz, with a usable range of about 0.2 to 4 THz. The strongest moment was found within 0.53 ps for three STFT windows and within 0.07 ps for the wavelet.
  • A weak feature at 50 to 65 ps sits about 35 to 41 dB below the main pulse in both methods. Its origin was not investigated.

Limits

No microtubule data was analysed. One recording, one run, no repeats. The file does not state its time unit, so picoseconds is an assumption, supported by the delay of the pellet pulses. Window sizes and the wavelet were chosen, not tuned. The 0.011 s timing is measured at 1 ms steps, so it is uncertain by roughly 10%. A sharp notch near 4.4 THz is not interpreted. Data: Leung, Ward-Berry, Wanvig i Dot, Lee and Zeitler, University of Cambridge, CC BY 4.0.
